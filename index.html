4

— about me

I've spent 4+ years on the kind of
problems that don't make it onto
most portfolios — because they're
hard to photograph.

Defence vehicles. Enterprise ERPs. AI tools for educators. Financial workflows. Telecom platforms. That's the actual list — not a niche, just wherever the complexity was real and the users couldn't afford bad design.

My degree was in electronics engineering. It probably explains why I think in systems before I think in screens. When I join a project, the first thing I want to understand isn't the UI — it's the environment. Who's using this, when, under what conditions, and what they've already given up trying to make work.

Research I've actually run: contextual interviews in operational environments, diary studies over weeks, usability evaluations, heuristic reviews, workflow mapping with stakeholders who'd never been in the same room. Not checkbox research. The kind that changes what you build.

I've led small teams — 2 designers here, 3 there — which means getting comfortable making calls without consensus, and defending them with something other than taste. Most of the products I've worked on are internal tools. Nobody tweets about them. But someone's job is easier because of them, and that's the whole point.

Looking for a full-time role where the design problems are genuinely hard — enterprise, AI, mission-critical, or some combination. If your product is the kind of thing users complain about at dinner, I'm probably interested.

How I Work
01

Research
First

Before sketching anything — contextual interviews, diary studies, workflow mapping. I need to understand the environment, not just the brief.

02

Systems,
Not Screens

I map the full ecosystem first. Enterprise UX breaks when design starts at the component level.

03

Iterate
in the Open

Wireframes go to engineers and stakeholders early. First review is a conversation, not a presentation. Decisions backed by evidence.

04

Design for
the Edge Case

The happy path is easy. I design for the distracted, time-pressured, error-prone user — especially in mission-critical environments.

05

Ship, Measure,
Revisit

Delivery isn't the end. Usability evaluation post-launch, learnings documented, next cycle informed.
